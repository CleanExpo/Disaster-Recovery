### Week 1 · Monday — "5 photos to take before a flood"

**Title:** Five photos to take BEFORE a flood (insurance gold)

**Body:**

If you live in a flood-prone suburb, the most useful five minutes you
will ever spend with your phone is this: walk through your home and
photograph the contents while everything is dry, intact, and where you
left it.

Why? Because when you lodge a claim after a flood, the loss adjuster
needs proof of what was there. Memory is fuzzy under stress; photos
are not.

Capture these five shots:

1. **Each room, wide angle** — corner-to-corner, including the floor
   and the ceiling.
2. **Inside cupboards and the pantry** — open the doors, take the photo.
3. **Whitegoods + electronics** — fridge, washing machine, TV, computer.
   Get the brand label visible.
4. **Floor coverings** — close-ups of carpet, timber, tiles. Note the
   room.
5. **Anything bespoke** — bookshelves, custom joinery, art, instruments.

Save them to cloud storage (not just the phone) so you can access them
even if the device is damaged.

If a flood does hit, our IICRC-certified contractor network can be on
site for make-safe and water extraction, where possible within 24 hours
of your claim.

### Week 1 · Wednesday — "What 'IICRC-certified' actually means"

**Title:** What "IICRC-certified" actually means (and why it matters)

**Body:**

You will see "IICRC-certified" on our site, in our posts, and on the
contractor profiles in the Disaster Recovery network. Here is what it
actually means.

The IICRC is the **Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration
Certification**. It is the international standards body that publishes
the technical standards Australian restoration tradespeople work to —
notably **S500** (water damage restoration) and **S520** (mould
remediation).

A contractor who is IICRC-certified has:

- Completed structured training in their damage category.
- Passed a written exam.
- Maintained their registration (it expires; lapsed certs are not valid).
- Agreed to a code of ethics that is enforceable by the IICRC.

The register is **public**. You can look up any contractor by name on
the IICRC website and confirm they hold a current registration.

Disaster Recovery only refers claims to contractors whose IICRC
registration we have verified on the public register. If the cert
lapses, the contractor doesn't get matched until it's renewed.

That's the "why it matters". The standards exist so the drying, the
mould remediation, and the documentation all hold up under insurer
review.

### Week 2 · Monday — "How to document storm damage for your insurer"

**Title:** How to document storm damage for your insurer (step-by-step)

**Body:**

After the storm passes and everyone is safe, your insurer will ask for
evidence. The quality of your documentation drives how smoothly your
claim moves.

Work through this list before anything is moved or thrown out:

1. **Date and time stamp** — your phone does this automatically; keep
   the metadata. Don't crop or re-save the originals.
2. **Wide shots first** — whole-of-property exterior, then each affected
   room interior.
3. **Close-ups of damage** — broken tiles, water ingress points, fallen
   trees, dented panels. Get the scale: put a hand or a ruler in frame.
4. **Pre-loss reference photos** — if you have any (insurance renewal
   photos, real-estate listing shots, family photos showing the room),
   keep them. They are gold for "this is what it looked like before".
5. **Receipts for emergency works** — tarps, board-up, tradies on the
   day. Keep every receipt; insurers usually reimburse reasonable
   make-safe costs.
6. **A written log** — date, time, what happened, what you did. Even a
   note in your phone's Notes app counts.

What NOT to do: don't throw out damaged items before the insurer has
seen photos. They might be repairable, or the insurer may want to
inspect.

If you need IICRC-certified contractors for make-safe (board-up, tarp,
water extraction), the Disaster Recovery network covers most of
Australia and New Zealand. Rapid response where possible.

**Suggested image:** Genuine storm-damaged Australian roof or window

### Week 2 · Wednesday — "Mould remediation — when DIY is dangerous"

**Title:** Mould — when DIY is fine, and when it isn't

**Body:**

A small patch of mould in the bathroom corner is not the same problem
as a wall full of black mould after a slow leak. Knowing the difference
matters because the second one can make people genuinely unwell.

**Probably fine to handle yourself:**

- Surface mould on tiles, grout, or silicone, less than about 1 m²
  total.
- A short-term cause that you've already fixed (a humid bathroom, a
  damp towel left for too long).
- No-one in the household has asthma, allergies, or a compromised
  immune system.

**Time to call IICRC-certified help (S520 standard):**

- Mould covering more than about 1 m², or growing on porous materials
  (plasterboard, insulation, carpet underlay, timber framing).
- The cause is unresolved (a slow plumbing leak, a rising-damp issue).
- Anyone in the household has a respiratory condition.
- The smell is musty and persistent — that usually means the
  mould is also somewhere you can't see.

The IICRC S520 standard exists because mould isn't just a cleaning
problem. Done wrong, you spread spores through the home. Done right
(containment, HEPA filtration, source-control, and proper disposal),
the property is restored and the underlying moisture problem is fixed.

If you're not sure which side of the line you're on, lodging a claim
gets you assessed by a certified contractor — no commitment to proceed.

**Suggested image:** A moisture meter being held against a wall, or a
contractor in PPE setting up a HEPA air scrubber. (Phill: equipment
photos work well here — see DR-447 brief.)

### Week 3 · Monday — "Sewage spills — keep people and pets away"

**Title:** Sewage spill at home? Keep everyone away — here's why

**Body:**

A sewage backup or main-line break inside a home is a Category 3
("black water") event under the IICRC S500 standard. That classification
exists because the water carries pathogens — bacteria, viruses, and
parasites that cause real illness.

**First steps, in order:**

1. **Get people and pets out of the affected area.** Don't walk through
   it. Don't let kids touch it. Don't let the dog drink it.
2. **Open windows** in adjacent areas (not the affected room, that just
   spreads aerosols).
3. **Turn off power to the affected area** at the switchboard if water
   has reached outlets or appliances.
4. **Don't try to mop it up yourself.** Domestic mops, towels, and
   household disinfectants don't address Cat 3 contamination — and
   they cross-contaminate every surface they touch afterwards.
5. **Photograph the scene** for your insurer (from a safe distance).
6. **Call your plumber** to stop the source.
7. **Lodge a claim** — IICRC-certified Cat 3 remediation requires
   PPE, biocides, controlled disposal of porous materials (carpet,
   underlay, plasterboard within the contamination zone), and
   post-remediation verification.

Materials in the contamination zone usually can't be salvaged —
that's a feature of the standard, not a contractor preference.
Restoring porous materials is unsafe.

If you're in this situation right now, lodge a claim and we'll
match you with a certified contractor. Rapid response where
possible.

**Suggested image:** Warning tape across a sectioned-off bathroom
or laundry, with PPE-clad contractor in background. Authentic

### Week 3 · Wednesday — "Fire damage — what gets thrown out vs cleaned"

**Title:** After a fire — what can be cleaned, and what has to go

**Body:**

Fire damage is rarely just the burnt thing. Smoke and soot travel
through HVAC systems, sit on every horizontal surface, and embed in
porous materials. Restoration after a fire is mostly about that
secondary damage.

**Generally cleanable (under the IICRC S700 fire damage standard):**

- Hard, non-porous surfaces — tiles, glass, sealed timber, metal.
- Most clothing and soft furnishings, with appropriate ozone or
  hydroxyl treatment.
- Sealed electronics, after specialised soot removal — assessed
  individually, not assumed.
- Many books and documents, via freeze-drying or specialist cleaning.

**Generally not salvageable:**

- Open food, opened pantry items, anything in cardboard.
- Cosmetics and medication.
- Mattresses and pillows that have absorbed smoke.
- Insulation in affected wall and ceiling cavities.
- Any material with direct flame contact.

The contractor's job is to triage, document for the insurer, and
preserve what can be preserved. The decision about what gets
disposed of is documented — that documentation matters because the
insurer covers the replacement cost only of items that genuinely
can't be restored.

If you've had a fire, the order of operations is: ensure everyone
is safe and the fire service has cleared the property → get a
make-safe contractor on site → photograph everything before any
clean-up starts → lodge the claim.

**Suggested image:** Soot-affected room being assessed by a contractor
with a clipboard or tablet — visible smoke staining on walls or
ceiling. Authentic photo only.

### Week 4 · Monday — "Affordability anxiety — your rights when insurer delays"

**Title:** Insurer dragging their feet? Know your rights

**Body:**

You've lodged a claim. The insurer has the photos, the police report,
the plumber's invoice — and three weeks later, you're still living in
a half-restored house. What now?

A few things worth knowing.

**Insurer obligations under the General Insurance Code of Practice
(2020):**

- A decision on a claim should ordinarily be made within **10 business
  days** of receiving all the information they reasonably need.
- For "extraordinary catastrophe" events (declared by the Insurance
  Council of Australia), the timeline can extend to 12 months — but
  the insurer must keep you informed.
- You have the right to a written explanation of any delay.
- You have the right to internal dispute resolution (IDR) within
  **30 calendar days** if you're unhappy with how the claim is being
  handled.

**If IDR doesn't resolve it:**

- You can escalate to the **Australian Financial Complaints Authority
  (AFCA)** — free, independent, binding on the insurer up to set
  monetary limits. afca.org.au.

**While you wait:**

- Keep documenting (photos, receipts, conversations, dates).
- If make-safe is genuinely needed to prevent further damage, most
  policies cover reasonable costs — keep the receipts.
- IICRC-certified contractors in the Disaster Recovery network can
  start make-safe before the full claim is approved, where the policy
  allows. The contractor invoices you directly; you submit to the
  insurer for reimbursement.

We don't act for the insurer or against them. We connect you to
certified people who do the actual work, and the work is documented
to the standard the insurer expects.

table, or a "claim documentation" folder/clipboard with a phone

### Week 4 · Wednesday — "How DR matches you with a contractor"

**Title:** Behind the scenes — how we match you with a contractor

**Body:**

When you lodge a claim through Disaster Recovery, what actually
happens? Here's the short version.

**Step 1 — Intake.** You submit the form at
`disasterrecovery.com.au/claim`. The fields capture what's needed
for matching: damage type, suburb and postcode, urgency, property
type, and your contact details. Privacy notice is shown at point of
collection (APP 5 compliant).

**Step 2 — Triage.** A real person reviews the claim. They confirm
the damage category (water, fire, mould, storm, sewage, biohazard,
trauma) and the urgency tier — emergency, urgent, or routine. That
classification drives the response window.

**Step 3 — Matching.** The system looks for contractors who:

- Hold a **current IICRC certification** in the relevant category
  (S500, S520, S700, or specialty as needed).
- Have **territory coverage** that includes your suburb.
- Are **available** — not currently at capacity.
- Hold **current insurance** (public liability, professional
  indemnity).

**Step 4 — Dispatch.** The matched contractor is notified. They
contact you to arrange the assessment and make-safe.

**Step 5 — Restoration.** The contractor scopes the works, agrees
the scope with you, and bills you directly. You submit the invoice
to your insurer (or, if the contractor and insurer have a direct
arrangement, the contractor handles that).

A few things we DON'T do: we don't bill on your behalf, we don't
take a cut of the restoration fee, we don't tell the insurer what
to approve. Our job ends when you're matched with a competent
certified contractor — which is the bit that's hardest to do alone
under stress.

**Suggested image:** A simple workflow diagram laid out on paper,
or a contractor and homeowner shaking hands on the front step of an
Australian house. Authentic photo preferred.
